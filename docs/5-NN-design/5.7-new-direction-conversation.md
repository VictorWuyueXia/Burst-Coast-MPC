## 1. Bang bang MPC

For input affine dynamics

$$
\dot x=f(x)+g(x)u,\qquad |u|\le u_{\max},
$$

Here $t$ is continuous time; $x(t)$ is the plant state; $\dot x$ is its time derivative; $u(t)$ is the commanded input; $f(x)$ is the uncontrolled drift dynamics; $g(x)$ maps the input into state motion; and $u_{\max}>0$ is the actuator magnitude limit. “Input affine” means that $u$ enters linearly through $g(x)u$, even though $f$ and $g$ may be nonlinear in $x$. Intuitively, $f$ describes how the plant moves on its own, while $g(x)u$ is the motion added by the actuator.

suppose the Hamiltonian depends linearly on $u$:

$$
\mathcal H_u=\sigma(t)u,
$$

Here $\mathcal H_u$ denotes the part of the optimal-control Hamiltonian that depends on the input, and $\sigma(t)$ is the scalar switching function assembled from the state, costate, and input channel. Its sign tells the optimizer which input extreme lowers the Hamiltonian; its magnitude measures how strongly one extreme is preferred.

where $\sigma(t)$ is the switching function determined by the state and costate. Minimizing the Hamiltonian gives

$$
u^*(t)=-u_{\max}\operatorname{sign}\sigma(t).
$$

Here $u^*(t)$ is the optimal input and $\operatorname{sign}(\sigma)$ is $+1$ for positive $\sigma$ and $-1$ for negative $\sigma$. The minus sign selects the actuator bound opposite to the switching-function sign, because that choice minimizes the linear product $\sigma u$.

Thus, the input switches between $+u_{\max}$ and $-u_{\max}$. This is bang bang control. A singular arc may occur where $\sigma=0$.

Adding an $L^1$ fuel penalty $\rho |u|$ creates a dead zone:

$$
u^*=
\begin{cases}
-u_{\max}\operatorname{sign}\sigma, & |\sigma|>\rho,\\
0, & |\sigma|<\rho.
\end{cases}
$$

Here $\rho>0$ is the weight on input usage, $|u|$ is instantaneous input magnitude, and $\rho|u|$ is the $L^1$ running penalty. The threshold compares the control benefit $|\sigma|$ with the usage price $\rho$: act at full magnitude only when the benefit exceeds the price; otherwise remain off. Equality $|\sigma|=\rho$ is a switching boundary at which the minimizing input may be non-unique.

This is bang off bang control and is closely connected to maximum hands off control. Under continuous time normality conditions, minimizing active duration through $L^0$ and minimizing $L^1$ input can produce the same solution set. See [Nagahara, Quevedo, and Nešić, IEEE TAC 2016](https://nagahara-masaaki.github.io/assets/pdfs/PS_File/TAC2016.pdf).

Your controller is not currently bang bang. During the burst, $v_i$ may take any bounded value, while zero input is imposed after $B$. Moreover, the $(\Delta u)^2$ term discourages sharp switching. Here $v_i$ is the continuously optimized input at burst step $i$, $\Delta u=u_i-u_{i-1}$ is the change between consecutive commands, and $(\Delta u)^2$ is its scalar squared smoothing penalty. Thus, unlike bang-bang control, the optimizer may choose intermediate amplitudes and is explicitly encouraged not to jump sharply between them.

## 2. Mixed integer programming

Mixed integer MPC places discrete modes and continuous controls inside one optimization. For example, define

$$
z_i\in\{0,1\},\qquad
-u_{\max}z_i\le u_i\le u_{\max}z_i.
$$

Here $i$ indexes prediction steps, $z_i$ is a binary actuator-enable decision, and $u_i$ is the continuous input at step $i$. Multiplying the input bound by $z_i$ couples the discrete mode to the continuous command: $z_i=0$ collapses both bounds to zero, whereas $z_i=1$ restores the full interval $[-u_{\max},u_{\max}]$.

Here $z_i=0$ forces $u_i=0$, while $z_i=1$ permits active control. Adding

$$
z_{i+1}\le z_i
$$

Because both variables are binary, this inequality permits $1\!\to\!1$, $1\!\to\!0$, and $0\!\to\!0$, but forbids $0\!\to\!1$. It therefore allows at most one enabled-to-disabled transition and prevents the actuator from restarting within the same event.

forces a single active prefix followed by a coast suffix. Then

$$
B=\sum_i z_i
$$

Here $B$ is the number of enabled prediction steps. For the monotone binary sequence above, summing its leading ones exactly recovers the burst length; multiplying $B$ by the sampling period would give the burst duration in seconds. $H$ denotes the total number of prediction steps until the next replan, so the coast length is $H-B$ steps.

## 3. Certifiable neural networks, SINDy, and α,β CROWN

“Alpha beta cron” almost certainly means **α,β CROWN**. SINDy and α,β CROWN solve different problems.

SINDy identifies explicit dynamics from data:

$$
\dot X\approx\Phi(X,U)\Xi,
$$

Here $X$ stacks sampled states, $U$ stacks the corresponding inputs, and $\dot X$ stacks estimated state derivatives. $\Phi(X,U)$ is the library matrix obtained by evaluating candidate functions at those samples, and $\Xi$ is the coefficient matrix mapping library functions to each state derivative. The approximation sign acknowledges measurement, differentiation, and model errors. Intuitively, SINDy searches for a short equation by setting most entries of $\Xi$ to zero.

where $\Phi$ is a finite dictionary such as

$$
\Phi=[1,\theta,\omega,u,\sin\theta,\theta\omega,\ldots],
$$

Here each entry denotes one candidate basis function: $1$ is a constant term, $\theta$ is pendulum angle, $\omega$ is angular velocity, $u$ is applied torque or command, $\sin\theta$ represents gravity's nonlinear angular dependence, and $\theta\omega$ is one possible interaction term. The ellipsis means other deliberately chosen candidates may be included; it is not an unspecified learned feature set.

and sparse regression forces most coefficients in $\Xi$ to zero. For the pendulum, an appropriate dictionary should recover

$$
\dot\theta=\omega,\qquad
\dot\omega=c_1\sin\theta+c_2\omega+c_3u.
$$

Here $\dot\theta$ is angular rate and therefore equals $\omega$; $\dot\omega$ is angular acceleration; and $c_1$, $c_2$, and $c_3$ are identified scalar coefficients for gravity, velocity-dependent damping, and actuator authority, respectively. Their signs and units depend on the angle and input conventions. Intuitively, the three retained terms say that acceleration is explained by gravity, damping, and applied actuation.

This is likely what your advisor meant by a “short finite basis.” More precisely, it is a finite candidate dictionary with only a few retained functions. SINDy provides interpretability, but ordinary SINDy does not certify approximation error, stability, or safety. See the [original SINDy paper](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1517384113).

α,β CROWN instead verifies a specified property of a trained neural network over an entire bounded input set. It propagates valid affine bounds through network activations. The $\alpha$ variables tighten these relaxations, while the $\beta$ variables incorporate activation splits within branch and bound. See the [β CROWN paper](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2021/hash/fac7fead96dafceaf80c1daffeae82a4-Abstract.html) and [official verifier](https://github.com/Verified-Intelligence/alpha-beta-CROWN).

For your critic, it could verify over a state region $\mathcal S$ that

$$
\widehat Q(s,a_i)+m\le \widehat Q(s,a_j),
\qquad
\forall s\in\mathcal S,\quad j\ne i,
$$

Here $s$ is any state in the certified region $\mathcal S$; $a_i$ is the candidate action intended to remain optimal; $a_j$ is any competing action; $\widehat Q(s,a)$ is the learned predicted cost-to-go for taking action $a$ at $s$; and $m>0$ is a required ranking margin. Because lower cost is better, the inequality says that $a_i$ beats every competitor by at least $m$ throughout the whole region, not only at sampled states.

where $m>0$ is a required margin. This proves that $a_i=(B_i,H_i)$ remains the selected grid action under bounded state uncertainty.

It does not prove that $\widehat Q=Q^*$, that MPC remains recursively feasible, or that the resulting closed loop is safe. Those require separate model error and control arguments. Because your pendulum dynamics are already known, SINDy is currently more relevant for critic distillation or residual dynamics than for replacing the plant model.

Here $Q^*$ is the true Bellman-optimal action-value function for the specified dynamics, constraints, event definition, and cumulative cost, whereas $\widehat Q$ is only the trained approximation. ## 3. Optimization problem

Use a generous but finite planning window $N$. Time is then a feasibility boundary, not the quantity being optimized:

$$

\text{subject to}\quad
&x_{k+1}=F(x_k,u_k),\\
&u_k=u_{\max}(p_k-n_k),\\
&x_k\in\mathcal X_{\mathrm{safe}},\\
&x_N\in\mathcal X_f,\\
&\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}y_k\le K_{\max}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $\mathbf x$, $\mathbf p$, $\mathbf n$, and $\mathbf y$ are the full planned sequences. $\mathcal X_{\mathrm{safe}}$ is the admissible state set, $\mathcal X_f$ is the required terminal set, and $K_{\max}$ is the activation budget. ## 5. Mixed integer model choice

## 6. What can realistically be certified

For an inverted equilibrium, a finite number of activations followed by permanent zero control cannot provide asymptotic stability. The uncontrolled upright equilibrium is unstable. You therefore need either:

1. A terminal controller whose corrections are not included in the transfer activation budget.
2. A naturally invariant terminal state under zero input.
3. A practical stability claim over a finite operating interval.

The strongest defensible formulation is:

> Minimum activation transfer into a certified terminal invariant set.

Let $\kappa_f(x)$ be the terminal controller and $V(x)$ its Lyapunov function. Certify over $\mathcal X_f$:

$$
V(x)>0,\qquad x\ne x^\star,
$$

$$
V\!\left(F(x,\kappa_f(x))\right)-V(x)
\le-c\|x-x^\star\|^2,
$$

$$
F(x,\kappa_f(x))\in\mathcal X_f.
$$

Here $x^\star$ is the desired equilibrium, $V(x)$ is a scalar Lyapunov candidate, $\kappa_f(x)$ is the terminal feedback law, $c>0$ is a required decrease rate, and $\|x-x^\star\|^2$ is squared state error. The first condition makes $V$ positive away from the goal; the second makes it decrease under terminal feedback; and the third makes $\mathcal X_f$ invariant, so the next state remains inside the certified terminal region.

For the burst coast phase, per step Lyapunov decrease is generally inappropriate because $V$ may increase during a coast. Use event level decrease instead:

$$

Here $j$ indexes replanning or activation events rather than simulation steps, $\rho$ is a contraction factor, and $\epsilon\ge0$ is an allowed residual caused by approximation or disturbance. The event-to-event value therefore contracts up to a fixed error floor, yielding practical rather than exact asymptotic convergence when $\epsilon>0$.

where $x_j$ and $x_{j+1}$ are consecutive activation or replanning states. With $\epsilon>0$, this certifies practical convergence at the event level.

| Property | DP table with interpolation | Distilled model |

| Representation | Explicit values at grid points | Neural network or another regressor |

## Direct interpolation

Suppose DP gives $V_{ij}=V_{\mathrm{DP}}(\theta_i,\omega_j)$. Bilinear interpolation estimates the value inside a grid cell:

$$

$$

Nonnegative weights that sum to one form a convex combination. Consequently, the interpolated value lies between the four corner values and reproduces a corner exactly when its weight is one.

This estimate depends only on the surrounding four grid points. It cannot invent global structure, which makes its error easier to understand. However, doubling the resolution of every dimension multiplies storage by $2^{n_x}$, where $n_x$ is the state dimension.

$$

This reversed inequality means distillation error has swapped the two candidates' order. It explains why policy agreement, top-$k$ recall, and closed-loop cost are more decision-relevant validation measures than value mean-square error alone.

Therefore, ranking accuracy and resulting closed loop cost matter more than value mean squared error.

## Recommendation for your project

Use both, with distinct roles:

1. Use the interpolated DP table as the authoritative offline baseline.
2. Distill $Q_{\mathrm{DP}}(x,B,H)$ when table lookup becomes memory intensive or higher dimensional.
3. Let the distilled model rank the candidate $(B,H)$ pairs.
4. Run exact nonlinear MPC for the best few candidates before execution.

For the current two dimensional pendulum, distillation is not necessary for computational feasibility. Its research value appears when demonstrating that the DP policy can be compressed and scaled while preserving feasibility, action ranking, minimum event layers, and closed loop performance.
